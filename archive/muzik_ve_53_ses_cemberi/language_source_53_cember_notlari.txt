The Pythagoreans saw numbers behind things that seemed unrelated, from musical intervals to geometric forms.

They began with 1, 2, 3 and 4. Their sum is 10, the Tetractys. More important were their ratios. 1:2 gives the octave, 2:3 the fifth and 3:4 the fourth. Musical harmony could be expressed through simple numerical relationships.

That suggested a wider principle. If harmony in sound depends on proportion, perhaps nature does too. The important thing was not the number alone, but the relationship between numbers.

The same four numbers were linked to geometry. 1 corresponded to the point, 2 to the line, 3 to the plane and 4 to the solid. A numerical sequence could describe the movement from unity to extended form.

Robert Fludd carried this Pythagorean tradition into the 1600s. He connected arithmetic with music, geometry, the human being and the cosmos. The human being was understood as a microcosm, reflecting the larger universe.

Language could also be brought into the system. Letters were assigned numerical values, allowing words to be examined through numerical relationships.

Fludd was not simply claiming that certain numbers were magical. He was searching for recurring proportions across different parts of reality. For him, the harmony of music, the structure of form and the order of the cosmos could be related through the same numerical principles.

Numbers were therefore not just measurements. They belonged to the structure of creation itself. #Numerlogy

🚨 A PHOTON CROSSED 2 BILLION LIGHT-YEARS… BUT IT SHOULD HAVE DISAPPEARED

Something extraordinary may have traveled across the universe and reached Earth when, according to our current understanding of physics, it should never have made it this far.

In 2022, astronomers detected the enormous gamma-ray burst known as GRB 221009A, an explosion roughly 2 billion light-years away. But hidden among the radiation from this distant event was something deeply puzzling: a photon-like signal with an estimated energy of about 300 teraelectronvolts (TeV).

That energy creates a serious problem. A photon this powerful should not be able to travel such a vast distance through space without being destroyed. Along its journey, it should have collided with the faint background light left over from the early universe. Such an interaction can produce an electron and a positron, effectively removing the photon from the journey.

Yet the Carpet detector at Russia’s Baksan Observatory recorded a high-energy event coming from the direction of GRB 221009A.

The signal arrived more than an hour after the initial burst.

So how could something that should have been absorbed by the universe survive for billions of years—and why did it arrive so late?

One possible answer takes scientists into territory far beyond established physics. Researchers have proposed that the photon might have temporarily changed into a hypothetical particle called an axion-like particle (ALP). Unlike an ordinary high-energy photon, an ALP could potentially travel through space without suffering the same kind of absorption. Somewhere along the way, it could then transform back into a photon before reaching Earth.

But there is a problem: calculations suggest that ALPs alone may not be enough to explain a 300 TeV photon surviving such an enormous journey.

The researchers therefore added another possibility—an extremely tiny violation of Lorentz invariance, one of the fundamental principles behind Einstein’s theory of special relativity. Some theories of quantum gravity predict that this principle might behave slightly differently at incredibly high energies.

Together, these ideas could potentially explain both the photon’s survival and its unusual delay.

But there is a major reason scientists are being cautious. This interpretation depends on essentially one extraordinary event, and the association between the detected signal and GRB 221009A still needs stronger confirmation.

So this is not proof that Einstein was wrong, nor proof that new physics has been discovered.

But if future observations find more photons behaving in the same strange way, scientists could gain an entirely new tool for studying the deepest laws of nature—and perhaps glimpse effects of quantum gravity at energies far beyond anything we can produce on Earth.

A tiny particle of light may have crossed billions of light-years carrying a message about physics that we have never been able to test before.

The mystery is not simply how the photon reached us. The bigger question is what it could be telling us about the universe itself.

The heart-shaped glow in a coffee cup is a caustic, a bright curve created when many reflected light rays concentrate along the same envelope. Incoming sunlight is nearly parallel, while the cup’s curved inner wall acts like a circular mirror. Each ray reflects according to the law of reflection.
